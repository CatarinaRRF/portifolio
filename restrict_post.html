The Long Way Around the Sentence

Why the seventh draft is often the first honest one — and how cutting the sentence you love is the surest way to hear the paragraph underneath it.

Nora Ashby 4 March 2026 9 minute read
Under review

The draft is not the essay

There is a particular sentence I keep in a folder called orphans. It is well made. It has a good rhythm and a small, surprising turn near the end, and for three years it belonged to an essay it very nearly ruined. I could not cut it because I could not stop admiring it. That, I have come to believe, is the whole problem of revision, compressed into a single line: the sentences that endanger a piece are almost never the bad ones. They are the ones you love.

I used to think revision was a matter of polishing — that a draft arrived roughly shaped, and the work was to sand it down until it shone. I no longer think this. Polishing is what you do in the last hour, and it is the least of it. The real work happens earlier and it feels less like sanding than like listening: reading the thing back until you can hear where it stops telling the truth.

A first draft is a set of instructions I leave for myself. It says: here is roughly what I think, in roughly this order, and here is where I lost my nerve. It is not the essay. It is a map of the territory where the essay might be, and like most maps it flattens exactly the things that matter — the slope of an argument, the weather of a mood, the moment where a reader's attention will, without warning, wander off.

So I have stopped grading first drafts. I write them badly on purpose and quickly, and I try not to read them for a day.1 The distance is not a luxury; it is the tool. What you cannot see in your own sentence at midnight becomes almost embarrassingly obvious at seven the next morning, in the one good hour of light the studio gets before the street wakes up.

You do not find the sentence by looking harder at it. You find it by walking away, letting the room go quiet, and coming back to see what is still standing. — from a note to myself, 2021

When I come back, I read aloud. This is not a flourish. The ear catches what the eye forgives: the clause that doubles back, the adjective doing a noun's job, the paragraph that ends one beat too late. If I stumble reading a line, the line is wrong — not my mouth. The body is a more honest editor than the mind, because it has no stake in being clever.

Cutting what you love

Here is the discipline I trust least and rely on most. When a passage feels precious — when I catch myself protecting it, arranging the rest of the paragraph to make room for it — I cut it. Not always for good. I paste it into the orphans folder, where it can keep being beautiful without doing any harm. But I take it out, and I read the paragraph again without it, and nine times in ten the paragraph exhales.

The reason is simple and slightly humbling. A sentence you love too much is usually load-bearing for your ego and nothing else. It is there because you want to be seen making it, not because the reader needs it to cross the room. Take it out and the structure holds — which tells you it was never structure. It was decoration you had mistaken for a wall.2

None of this is fast. A page can take a morning; a paragraph can take a week of mornings. But slowness, here, is not inefficiency. It is the medium. The essay is not hiding behind a cleverer method or a better app. It is waiting, patiently, in the seventh draft, for you to stop performing and start telling the truth — which, as it turns out, usually requires fewer words than the performance did.

A short practice

If any of this is useful, it reduces to three small habits: write the first draft too fast to admire; leave it long enough to forget; read it aloud in the morning and believe your stumbles. The rest is just courage, applied one sentence at a time, and the willingness to keep a folder of orphans you were brave enough to set down.3

The orphan I mentioned at the start is still in the folder. I read it now and then. It is still well made, and I am still fond of it, and it is still doing no harm to anyone. That, I think, is the kindest thing you can do for a sentence you love: let it be good somewhere it isn't needed, so the essay can be true where it is.

Footnotes

  1. A day is the minimum. For anything longer than an essay I've learned to wait a week — the errors I can't see on Tuesday are unmissable by the following Monday. ↩
  2. I owe the wall-versus-decoration idea, loosely, to years of reading Vivian Gornick on the difference between the situation and the story. She would put it far better, and in fewer words. ↩
  3. The orphans folder is thirty-one files deep as of this morning. I have never once regretted moving a sentence into it, and only twice moved one back out. ↩
