Beneath the Moss Carpets of a Cerrado Gallery Forest

A survey of moss-associated bacteria at the Roncador Ecological Reserve near Brasília reveals a striking split

Catarina de Freitas 31 August 2026 12 minute read

Mosses look like passive scenery - thin green mats clinging to tree trunks, easy to walk past without a second glance. But each mat has its own microscopic ecosystem, called a bryosphere, hosting bacterial communities that remain poorly catalogued outside temperate biomes. In the Brazilian Cerrado, where gallery forests thread through savanna along riverbanks, almost nothing has been published about who lives in these moss carpets at the microbial level.

That's the gap a recent survey set out to close. Researchers from UnB, collected six moss samples — three from Isopterygium tenerum and three from Erythrodontium squarrosum — growing on tree trunks at the headwaters of the Pitoco creek inside the Roncador Ecological Reserve (RECOR), a protected pocket of Cerrado chosen specifically to minimize human disturbance.

From Swab to Sequence

The wet-lab side is straightforward to summarize: total DNA extraction, Illumina MiSeq sequencing of the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene, and bioinformatic processing in QIIME2, with taxonomy assigned against the SILVA 138 reference database. What lands on the analyst's desk after that is a matrix — in this case, 662 rows of ASVs across 14 columns, six of them holding read counts for samples labeled DF01 through DF06.

One quiet but important detail shaped everything downstream: the metadata never states which samples belong to which moss species. So before any ecology could be discussed, the data itself had to be interrogated for structure.

The first pass was housekeeping. Of the 662 ASVs, one had no domain-level assignment at all ("Unassigned"), and eleven were organellar contamination — 8,527 chloroplast reads and 134 mitochondrial reads, both expected byproducts of sequencing plant-associated material. All twelve were dropped, along with the now-redundant SILVA 138 identifier column, leaving a clean bacteria-only table for analysis.

results.py 
1asv_table <- asv_table %>%
                  2  filter(Domain == "Bacteria") %>%          # drop 1 Unassigned ASV
                  3  filter(!Class %in% c("Chloroplast",        # drop 11 organellar ASVs
                  4                        "Mitochondria"))
                  5
                  6asv_table <- asv_table %>% select(-Database)  # SILVA 138 column now redundant

Letting the Data Sort Itself

With six unlabeled samples on hand, the analysis leaned on ordination to recover structure the metadata withheld. A Principal Coordinates Analysis on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, computed with the vegan package (v. 2.6-10) in R (v. 4.3.1), split the samples cleanly into two clusters. DF01, DF02, and DF03 sat close together in ordination space; DF04, DF05, and DF06 were more dispersed from one another but still distinct from the first trio. The first axis alone explained 78.6% of the variance, the second 10.2% — a strong signal for just two ordination axes.

That clustering, combined with the order samples were listed in the original dataset, supports treating DF01–DF03 and DF04–DF06 as two sets of biological replicates — plausibly corresponding to the two moss species, though without explicit metadata confirming host assignment, the analysis stops short of naming names. Instead, the two groups are simply Sample A (DF01–DF03) and Sample B (DF04–DF06).

Before trusting any diversity comparison between them, sequencing depth needed a sanity check. Rarefaction curves for both groups reached a clear asymptote well before the maximum read depth, indicating additional sequencing would have turned up few if any new ASVs — the sampling effort was sufficient for the comparisons that followed.

The two groups didn't just cluster differently — they told almost opposite ecological stories.

Sample A produced 159,202 bacterial reads resolving into 237 distinct taxa. Sample B produced 152,551 reads but resolved into 513 taxa — more than double the richness from a comparable sequencing effort. Across both groups combined, 649 unique taxa were detected.

Alpha-diversity metrics, calculated with the Shannon–Wiener index (H′) and Simpson's index (expressed as 1 − D), sharpen the contrast:

Pooled, Sample A returns a Shannon index of 3.082 and a Simpson's index of 0.1815 — moderate diversity with a strong dominance signature. Sample B returns a Shannon index of 4.4683 and a Simpson's index of just 0.0517, consistent with a community that is both richer and far more even, with no single taxon lording over the rest.

The taxon driving that dominance in Sample A has a name: Abditibacterium utsteinense. It alone accounted for 21,980 reads — an outsized share of the group's total — with two uncharacterized Pirellula ASVs trailing behind it. Abditibacterium shows up again at the top of Sample B, with 7,101 reads, but this time it shares the podium: a WWE3-affiliated bacterium contributed 3,996 reads, and Aquihabitans daechungensis added 1,424. Same genus leading both communities, very different degree of monopoly.

At the phylum level (visualized as stacked relative-abundance bar plots built with ggplot2 v. 3.5.2, retaining only taxa above 1% of total reads per sample), Abditibacteriota dominates both sample groups — unsurprising given Abditibacterium's standing — followed by Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, Planctomycetota, and Patescibacteria. Drilling into Proteobacteria specifically, Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria are the most abundant classes, alongside Planctomycetes, Verrucomicrobiae, and Bacteroidia. At order level, Abditibacteriales, Pirellulales, WWE3, Microtrichales, Burkholderiales, and Xanthomonadales lead; at family level, Abditibacteriaceae, Pirellulaceae, WWE3, Comamonadaceae, and Hydrogenedensaceae are most frequent.

What It Adds Up To

Two moss mats growing meters apart, in the same protected patch of gallery forest, host bacterial communities that diverge sharply in both structure and evenness — one shaped by a near-monopoly, the other spread across hundreds of coexisting taxa. The genus tying both stories together, Abditibacterium, is still a relatively obscure lineage in the literature; its consistent dominance across both moss hosts here is a reasonable hint that it plays some functional role in this bryosphere, though pinning that down is a question for follow-up work — ideally with metadata that finally links each sample explicitly to its host moss species.

For now, the analysis stands as a reminder that even "background" habitats like moss mats on tree bark carry real ecological structure — and that sometimes the most informative move an analyst can make is asking the ordination to sort out what the metadata left unsaid.

Footnotes

  1. Analysis conducted in R (v. 4.3.1) using vegan (v. 2.6-10) for diversity and ordination, and ggplot2 (v. 3.5.2) for visualization. Taxonomic classification via QIIME2 against the SILVA 138 reference database.
